What Happens When You Call 311 About a Homeless Individual?

An exploration of how New York City's Department of Homeless Services and NYPD responded to 311 complaints related to homelessness.

NYC OpenData Tableau 311 Data

If you come across a person experiencing homelessness and want to offer help, one option you have is to call 311 or make a request online. These requests are sent to the Department of Homeless Services or the NYPD, who then respond according to the information provided.

But what happens once your request is submitted? What efforts are made to assist the unhoused individual? Are the efforts by DHS and the NYPD effective in getting the person help? Should you even make that 311 call?

The following visualizations explore these questions by looking at how each agency “resolves” homelessness-related 311 requests and how long those resolutions take. The project uses NYC 311 complaint data categorized as Encampment, Homeless Encampment, Homeless Street Condition, and Homeless Person Assistance.

How Long Does It Take to Resolve a 311 Homeless Complaint?

This line graph compares DHS and the NYPD by the time it took each agency to “resolve” homelessness-related 311 complaints. In the data analyzed for this project, the NYPD consistently resolved complaints more quickly than DHS.

One possible explanation is that the agencies respond to different types of complaints. The NYPD dealt with Homeless Street Condition and Homeless Encampment complaints, while DHS responded to Homeless Person Assistance requests, which directly involve an individual and may take more time to resolve.

To calculate average resolution time, I measured the difference between the Resolution Action Updated Date and the Created Date. I excluded several extreme outliers — including one average of 89 days — so that smaller changes over time remained readable.

Source: NYC OpenData

NYPD Response to Homeless Complaints

This bar graph shows NYPD responses to Homeless Street Condition and Homeless Encampment 311 complaints. I grouped the resolution descriptions according to whether the response appeared to provide recourse.

The results showed that the NYPD frequently reported finding no violation. That outcome appeared approximately twice as often as reports that the agency “took action to fix the condition.” The responses raised questions for me about what it means for these complaints to be considered resolved and how transparent that process is to the person who submitted the request.

I chose a bar graph to make the difference between the grouped outcomes easy to compare. Similar Resolution Action Descriptions were grouped together, while descriptions that did not contribute meaningfully to the research question were excluded.

Source: NYC OpenData

DHS Resolutions to Homeless Person Assistance 311 Requests

In the data analyzed for this project, DHS most frequently reported that it could not find the individual. When an individual was found, the data frequently recorded that assistance was declined.

Of the 62,537 Homeless Person Assistance requests included in my analysis, only five were recorded as resulting in an individual accepting assistance. The dataset cannot tell us why DHS could not locate many individuals, why assistance was declined, or what happened to an individual after the reported resolution.

I used a packed bubble chart to emphasize the relative size of the different outcomes. Similar Resolution Action Descriptions were grouped together to reduce the number of categories and make the comparison easier to read.

For example, cases in which DHS determined the issue was outside its jurisdiction, referred the request elsewhere, or reported insufficient information were grouped into a category I called “No response from DHS.”

Source: NYC OpenData

Next Steps

One next step would be to compare average resolution time with the way a complaint was submitted — mobile, online, or phone — to see whether one method is associated with faster resolution.

Another direction would be geographic. Looking at the locations of homelessness-related 311 complaints could show whether some neighborhoods generate more complaints, which agency responds, and whether average resolution time differs across boroughs.

A more complex next step would be to examine what data the NYPD and DHS collect during these interactions and how that information is subsequently used.

Conclusion

So, what happens after someone makes the call?

This project began with a fairly simple question about what happens after a person submits a homelessness-related request to 311. The data showed substantial differences between how DHS and the NYPD handled those requests and raised further questions about what a “resolved” complaint actually means.

My analysis also suggested that relatively few of these requests resulted in the kind of direct assistance a caller might assume they are initiating. Based on those findings, I questioned whether reporting an unhoused individual through 311 was necessarily an effective way to offer that person help.
